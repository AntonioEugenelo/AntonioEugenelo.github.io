Misfortunes Never Come Singly: Monetary Policy after Sequential Shocks

Shocks rarely wait for the economy to return to steady state. This paper develops path log-linearisation, which approximates a model around the nonlinear transition left by an earlier shock rather than around the steady state, and uses it to ask how that inherited transition changes the Phillips curve and the optimal response to the next shock.

  • Draft in preparation

The last few years have delivered shocks in close succession: pandemic lockdowns, fiscal stimulus, supply-chain disruptions, the Russian invasion of Ukraine, rounds of tariffs. When a new shock arrives, inflation, marginal costs, capital and expectations are often still far from their steady-state values, so the trade-offs facing the central bank may depend on the transition already underway. Standard log-linearisation removes this history dependence by construction.

I propose path log-linearisation: a local approximation taken around the exact deterministic path generated by a first shock, and evaluated in the direction of a second, unanticipated one. The result is a linear model with time-varying coefficients, including a path-dependent Phillips curve whose slope and shock loadings depend on the economy’s recent history. Building on the linear-quadratic approach of Benigno and Woodford (2012), I characterise the conditions under which the reference path supports a valid quadratic welfare approximation, so that optimal policy can be studied as a continuation problem along the path.

Applied to New Keynesian models with and without capital and to Iacoviello (2005), current simulations point to a flatter local Phillips curve after inflationary shocks. In preliminary experiments, the optimal interest-rate response to a second cost-push shock is up to one-third smaller on impact than steady-state analysis would prescribe.

Government Spending in Disaggregated Economies

When government purchases move the prices consumers pay, spending enters the Phillips curve and steepens it by as much as 25%. The optimal fiscal rule becomes less counter-cyclical, and pro-cyclical in sectors with flexible prices. If politics fixes total spending, the planner still reallocates across sectors, and optimal policy becomes relative.

  • Draft, October 2026

Effective stabilisation of asymmetric shocks requires understanding the sectoral dynamics through which fiscal and monetary policies transmit in disaggregated economies. Multi-sector New-Keynesian frameworks are well suited to this task, yet they often rely on two restrictive assumptions that this paper relaxes. First, government demand does not enter firms’ consumer-price-setting problem; we drop this assumption by setting a pass-through parameter κ ∈ [0, 1], nesting the original framework at κ = 0. Second, government spending at both the aggregate and sectoral level is entirely under the planner’s control; we assume that the aggregate volume of government goods is exogenously set, reflecting political constraints, while the planner can flexibly allocate spending across sectors.

Allowing for pass-through of government demand on consumer prices introduces government spending into the Phillips curve and steepens its slope by as much as 25%. The optimal fiscal rule becomes less counter-cyclical and turns pro-cyclical in sectors with high price flexibility. Assuming an exogenously set aggregate government good provision, optimal fiscal policy is characterised by relative (rather than absolute) sectoral interventions. Our results underscore the critical roles of government demand pass-through to prices and politically set aggregate government good provision in shaping optimal fiscal policy.

Tariffs, Production Networks, and Spillovers: The Case of a US–China Trade War

With Pablo Aguilar, Matthieu Darracq Pariès, Alistair Dieppe, Rubén Domínguez-Díaz, José-Elías Gallegos and Javier Quintana

ECB Working Paper Series No 3254, July 2026

A reciprocal 10-point US–China tariff leaves the tariff-imposing country with more of the inflation and the targeted country with more of the output loss; production networks amplify the contraction, and dollar invoicing deepens it in China.

  • PDF
  • SSRN

We study the short-run macroeconomic transmission of a US–China tariff war in an open economy multi-sector New Keynesian model with input–output linkages, sectoral nominal rigidities, and heterogeneous currency invoicing. A reciprocal 10 percentage-point tariff increase generates asymmetric incidence: the tariff-imposing country bears more of the inflationary burden, while the targeted country experiences the larger output contraction. Production networks amplify this contraction by propagating the shock beyond the directly tariffed bilateral margin. Currency invoicing further shapes transmission. Under heterogeneous invoicing, dollar-priced border prices weaken the expenditure-switching role of exchange rates, deepening the contraction in China relative to producer-currency pricing and altering third-country spillovers. The EA response is small in the aggregate, but only because positive trade-diversion margins are offset by weaker demand from China and multilateral adjustments.

We then exploit the model’s sectoral structure by imposing tariffs on one Chinese sector at a time. Sectoral incidence is highly concentrated, but aggregate effects cannot be inferred from the directly tariffed sector alone: domestic propagation offsets own-sector gains in the US, reinforces own-sector losses in China, and leaves the EA as a net object shaped by opposing trade margins. The results show that tariff incidence depends jointly on where the tariff lands, how the shock propagates through production networks, and how invoicing governs border-price adjustment. A framework that combines these margins delivers a materially different assessment from one built on bilateral trade shares alone.

No-Poaching Clauses in Italian Franchise Agreements: Initial Findings

Published in Economia & Lavoro. Earlier version: Nuffield Economics Discussion Paper 2024-W04

Six of the seven Italian franchise agreements I obtained contain no-poaching clauses, the contract terms linked to lower wages in the US; I argue their removal would raise wages more in the EU than it did there.

  • Discussion paper
  • Journal version

In 2017, following a paper by Ashenfelter and Krueger, No-Poaching Clauses (NPCs) in the Quick Service Restaurant Industry rose to the centre of the US Antitrust debate. Several papers have since estimated a significant negative impact of NPCs on wages. The debate on such clauses has yet to reach the EU, mainly due to a lack of evidence of their existence. A “civic access” to documents used in a legal proceeding allowed the acquisition of seven Italian franchise agreements entered into by a major international franchisor, six of which clearly enforce NPCs. This article discusses the main features and governance implications of these agreements, analyses the differences between EU and US competition case law and labour markets, and argues that NPC removal would raise wages in the EU more than it did in the US.
